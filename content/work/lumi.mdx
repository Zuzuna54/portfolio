## Two requirements in tension

A companion has to remember you. Not "has your name in a system prompt" — actually remember, across
months, the way a person does. And it has to answer fast enough that talking to it feels like
talking, not like querying.

Memory pushes toward more context. Latency pushes toward less. Almost every design decision here
is somewhere on that line.

This one is entirely my own company, so unlike the other two studies I can say whatever I like
about it.

## Four layers of memory

Short-term context in Redis. Episodic facts in Postgres. Emotional state tracked separately. A
long-term relationship graph in a graph store.

They're split because they have different access patterns and different lifetimes, not because
layering is tidy. Short-term context is read every turn and thrown away. Episodic facts are
written rarely and read on recall. The relationship graph is traversed, not scanned — which is
precisely what a graph store is good at and a relational store is not.

## The choice I'd defend

Every new session **reprocesses the full history of prior sessions**. There is no retention
window and no ceiling.

This is expensive and it is the whole product. The failure mode I was designing against is the one
every long-lived assistant has: it works beautifully for a month, then the window slides, and it
quietly starts forgetting the thing you told it in week two. Nobody notices the moment it happens.
They just notice, eventually, that it doesn't know them anymore.

A fixed window makes that certain. Reprocessing makes recall at month nine the same shape as
recall at week one.

The cost is real — session start is heavier, and it grows with history. That's the trade I took,
and it's why the memory layers are split by access pattern: the reprocessing path only has to walk
what's actually relevant, not everything ever stored.

A separate subsystem of background agents reads conversation metadata and evolves the character
configuration over time, so personality drifts with the relationship rather than being static
across it.

## Under 500ms

Streaming end to end — streaming speech-to-text into the model into custom text-to-speech. Nothing
waits for a complete result before starting the next stage.

That number is the difference between conversation and transaction. Past roughly half a second,
people start behaving like they're using a computer: they over-enunciate, they wait, they stop
interrupting. Under it, they just talk.

## Isolation and hardware

The backend is licensed to third-party brands as a white-label platform, which makes per-device
memory separation a hard requirement rather than a nice property. Postgres row-level security
enforces it — one brand's devices can never reach another's, and that's true at the database
rather than by convention in application code.

On the hardware side: custom ESP32-S3, low-power, cloud-first, from concept to first functional
module in under four weeks. Speed there came from deciding early that the device would be thin —
capture, stream, play — and that everything interesting would live in the cloud.

## What I'd do differently

Reprocessing full history is the right default and the wrong *only* option. There should be a
tier — a cheap path for short sessions that doesn't pay the full cost, escalating to full
reprocessing when the conversation goes somewhere that needs it. I built the expensive path first
because it's the one that proves the idea, but production wants both.
